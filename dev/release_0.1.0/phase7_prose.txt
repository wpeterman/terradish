# terradish <img src="man/figures/terradish-sticker.png" align="right" height="200"/>

[![DOI](https://zenodo.org/badge/DOI/10.5281/zenodo.21225712.svg)](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21225712)

terradish estimates landscape conductance surfaces from genetic data by maximum likelihood on a raster graph. It fits log-linear conductance models and can optionally estimate each covariate's Gaussian smoothing scale and spline shape. Models fit pairwise genetic distances through the MLPE likelihood, or allele-frequency covariance and squared distances derived from it through a Wishart likelihood on site contrasts. Both families accept the same pairwise environmental covariates alongside resistance. Exact sparse Cholesky and algebraic multigrid solvers handle large rasters. Fixed-domain spatial cross-validation compares conductance formulas.

Estimated coefficients describe relative conductance and are conditional on the measurement-model terms. They do not directly estimate movement, migration, or causal landscape effects. For Wishart likelihoods, the user-supplied information parameter `nu` controls inferential precision. Using the SNP count overstated information by roughly an order of magnitude in the package's forward-time validation experiments.

## Installation

The supported core requires landgraph version 0.0.3 or later. Install a compatible companion version before terradish:



For local development with both repositories checked out side by side, install landgraph from its checkout first. The terradish CRAN release depends on that companion version being available on CRAN.

## Quick start



Check convergence before interpreting the surface. Code 0 meets the stopping rule; code 1 reaches the iteration limit; code 2 reports a stall or failed line search. Also inspect `fit$fit$subproblem` for nuisance optimization. Numerical convergence does not establish model adequacy.

## Interpreting estimates

For a standardized linear covariate, `exp(coef(fit))` is the conductance multiplier for a one-standard-deviation increase, holding other terms fixed. A common conductance scale is absorbed by the measurement model's resistance coefficient, so absolute conductance is unidentified.

Gaussian `sigma` describes raster smoothing in map units. The smoothed layer is re-standardized at every candidate sigma, so its coefficient is per standard deviation of that layer. The half-cell lower bound still smooths, and the upper bound is limited by the retained kernel window. Dispersal averaging can favor positive scales without a separate ecological scale of effect. Use `gaussian_scale_summary()`, `summary(fit)$sigma_table`, and `gaussian_scale_profile()` to examine units, bounds, and uncertainty.

Spline models are unpenalized regression splines with fixed degrees of freedom. Their centered bases and knots are retained for prediction. `summary(fit)$spline_monotonicity` describes shape over the focal-site covariate range. Tails outside that range are poorly identified.

| Response | Measurement model | Main requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Pairwise genetic distances, including ratio-estimator FST | `mlpe` or `leastsquares` | A defensible mean structure; MLPE models shared-site correlation |
| Allele-frequency covariance | `wishart_covariance` | Coherent covariance and explicit effective information |
| Squared distances derived from covariance | `generalized_wishart` | Admissible geometry and explicit effective information |

The two Wishart forms use the same site-contrast likelihood for matching covariance and distance representations. Passing `check_distance_response()` does not give an FST ratio estimator a Wishart sampling model. Grouped covariance should use landgraph's coherent `gower` diagonal; `within` is on a different scale. Rare-variant weighting and unequal group sizes require sensitivity checks.

### What `nu` changes

Wishart point estimates are invariant to rescaling `nu` at the optimum. Standard errors scale as `1 / sqrt(nu)`, while likelihood-ratio statistics and the likelihood contribution to information criteria scale with `nu`. Linkage and shared population history can make effective information much smaller than the marker count.

Use `terradish_rescale_nu(fit, nu = ...)` to report conclusions over plausible values. Dividing `nu` by 20 increases standard errors by `sqrt(20)` without refitting estimates. AIC at nominal marker information can over-select environmental terms and flexible curves. Use spatial CV for predictive support and rescaling for inferential sensitivity.

## Combine IBE and IBR



The IBE:IBR ratio expresses an environmental difference in resistance-distance units with a joint uncertainty interval. Raw coefficients are not directly comparable across families. Ratios require matching transforms and scaling and are undefined when the resistance coefficient is zero.

Environmental terms can reflect assortative mating, dispersal filtering, local adaptation, or environmental patterns in site variance. Report conductance fits with and without pairwise terms because signs can change. A uniform `~ 1` surface with environmental terms represents **IBD + IBE**, not IBE alone.

## Model comparison

`terradish_folds()` constructs folds; `terradish_cv_folds()` keeps the landscape graph fixed while withholding sites. It accepts per-formula conductance factories and repeated folds. Reprofiled nuisance parameters compare conductance formulas under one measurement model. Set `nuisance = "fixed"` to retain every measurement parameter from training and score predictive densities when comparing measurement extensions.

Read success counts first. Failed folds make full totals unavailable, and rankings use only folds successful for every candidate. Paired differences and their standard errors are descriptive because folds share data. Checkpoints reject incompatible input or model changes.

MLPE and Wishart scores do not share a common predictive scale. Within one Wishart comparison, changing `nu` rescales scores without changing ranks when the same folds succeed. Likelihood-ratio tests require nested models, matching responses and graphs, and fixed `nu`. Environmental Wishart weights need a boundary reference, not an ordinary chi-square test.

## Prediction and large landscapes

New-landscape prediction reuses fitted log-linear terms, spline knots and centers, or Gaussian post-smoothing scaling. Apply stored input scaling with `scale_covariates(new_rasters, reference = original_scaled_rasters)` before building a new graph. Slim fits retain covariance and interval methods but cannot predict after their model closures are removed.

`solver = "auto"` selects AMG above the large-graph threshold regardless of the number of right-hand sides. AMG iteration counts can increase with conductance contrast. Experimental parallel derivatives use PSOCK workers for Hessian and partial solves; AMG and cached CHOLMOD ignore `cores`.

Cropping changes resistance. One audit found increases of 15–23% with a two-cell buffer and 1–2% with ten cells. These are case-specific results: compare larger-buffer refits. Landmark and coarse-raster starts require exact refinement; `terradish_grid()` supports exact evaluation only. `terradish_assess_settings()` is a speed probe, not a convergence or statistical check.

## Experimental features

Directed conductance, hierarchical fields, site-specific drift terms, pair subsets, Kron reduction, block-CG, and legacy CV remain on the `experimental` branch. Their unresolved limitations are documented in that branch's `EXPERIMENTAL.md`.



## Changes in 0.1.0

- The supported core concentrates on log-linear, Gaussian, and spline conductance with MLPE and contrast Wishart likelihoods.
- Graphs use eight directions by default. Gaussian alignment, kernel bounds, spline prediction, and environmental kernels are corrected.
- Convergence records, covariance and interval methods, Gaussian profiles, IBE ratios, and `nu` sensitivity are available.
- Fixed-domain CV supports per-formula factories, fixed nuisance prediction, repeated folds, failure accounting, and compatible checkpoints.
- Stored scaling, new-landscape prediction, combined pairwise covariates, and `nu_fit` sensitivity are supported.
- Research prototypes and unsupported solvers have moved off the supported core. See `NEWS.md` for migration details.

## Vignettes and attribution

Start with `vignette("getting-started", package = "terradish")`. Other guides cover comparison, IBE and IBR, Wishart covariance, splines, Gaussian scales, large landscapes, and simulation design. Open the collection with `browseVignettes("terradish")`.

Nate Pope developed the sparse graph optimization, reverse-mode derivatives, MLPE, and generalized Wishart foundations in [radish](https://github.com/nspope/radish). terradish extends that framework with terra-native workflows and the supported features above. Selected deprecated `radish*` wrappers remain; full compatibility with every historical entry point is not promised.

## Why optimize sigma inside the model?

The original `radish` workflow for raster smoothing usually worked in two steps:

1. Smooth each raster at several candidate scales.
2. Fit a resistance model to each smoothed raster surface and compare the fits.

That approach is still useful, but it treats scale as an **outer** tuning
parameter. The new native Gaussian conductance system in `terradish` moves the
scale parameter into the conductance model itself, so the optimizer can update:

- the conductance coefficient(s), and
- the smoothing scale(s)

in the same likelihood calculation.

This vignette shows the current implementation of that idea using
`gaussian_smoothed_loglinear_conductance()`.

---

## What sigma means here

For a raster covariate `x`, the Gaussian scale-aware model first smooths the
raster with a Gaussian kernel and then uses the smoothed values in the usual
log-linear conductance model.

Conceptually:

$$C_i = \exp(\beta \cdot x_{i,\sigma})$$

where:

- $x_{i,\sigma}$ is the Gaussian-smoothed raster value at cell *i*,
  standardized over retained cells after smoothing
- $\beta$ is the conductance coefficient
- $\sigma$ controls how broad the smoothing kernel is

Small `sigma` means that the raster is averaged over a narrow neighborhood.
Large `sigma` means that it is averaged over a broader neighborhood. The
coefficient `beta` describes a one-standard-deviation change in that smoothed
layer, with standardization recomputed at each trial scale. Its magnitude
therefore needs to be interpreted jointly with `sigma`. Here,
`sigma` is a Gaussian smoothing scale in the raster's map units. It is not an
estimate of dispersal distance, home-range size, movement distance, or the
spatial scale of a causal ecological process. Dispersal can average exposure
over a neighborhood, so a fitted positive scale may reflect that averaging
even when the underlying ecological response is local.

### Are there limits on sigma?

Yes. `terradish` now estimates `sigma` directly on its natural distance scale
and fits it within explicit lower and upper bounds.

By default, those bounds are data-derived:

- the lower bound is half of the mean raster cell width
- the upper bound is `min(nrow(raster), ncol(raster)) * mean(res(raster)) / 6`,
  approximately one-sixth of the smaller raster dimension for square cells

You can override those defaults with `sigma_lower =` and `sigma_upper =` when
you create the conductance model. The kernel is truncated to a raster-sized
window. The default upper bound keeps approximately three standard deviations
inside half the smaller dimension; larger requested bounds issue a truncation
warning. Near that upper bound the covariate can become almost constant, so
scale and effect size may be weakly identified. The half-cell lower bound
still smooths and is not equivalent to an unsmoothed raster.

Internally, the optimizer now works on a converted sigma scale by default,
using approximate cell-width units. The fitted coefficients and summaries are
still reported back in map units for users, so the conversion helps the
optimization without changing the interpretation target.

Two practical caveats are worth keeping in mind:

- factor-valued raster layers are rejected for this Gaussian conductance model
  rather than being treated as scalable predictors
- leverage / `partial_X` diagnostics are still unavailable, because the current
  diagnostic machinery assumes a one-to-one match between conductance
  parameters and design-matrix covariates

## Worked example

We will fit a small staged example using the `melip` data. To keep the vignette
fast and stable, we will:

- use only eight sampling sites
- start with one raster covariate (`forestcover`)
- use `mlpe` as the measurement model, because the response matrix contains
  non-independent pairwise genetic distances

This is enough to show the practical workflow without making the vignette slow.
The main goal here is not to maximize model complexity. Instead, we will use
the staged workflow that is usually safest for real data:

1. fit a one-raster Gaussian scale model
2. compare it to a standard fixed-raster model
3. only then add a second raster and inspect whether the fitted scales still
   behave sensibly



### Step 1: Build a conductance surface with the original raster

Unlike the standard fixed-raster workflow, we do **not** pre-scale the raster
with `scale_covariates()` here. The Gaussian conductance model smooths the raw
raster first, then standardizes the active-cell values internally at each
parameter evaluation.



Two details matter here:

- `saveStack = TRUE` is required, because the Gaussian conductance model needs
  the retained raster stack as its smoothing domain.
- We are fitting a smoothing scale for the raster itself, so the original
  raster values need to be available.

### Step 2: Create the Gaussian scale-aware conductance model



This conductance model will estimate two parameters:

- `forestcover`: the conductance coefficient
- `sigma.forestcover`: the Gaussian smoothing scale on the natural distance scale

The same mechanism also supports interactions and polynomial terms after
smoothing, but it is usually better to start with the simplest one-raster model
you can justify.

### Step 3: Fit the one-raster Gaussian scale model

`optimizer = "auto"` currently resolves to BFGS for this conductance model.
That is intentional: the native Gaussian scale path supports analytic
gradients, and BFGS is the stable first optimizer for the joint
`beta + sigma` parameter vector.



### Optional speed-up: coarse-raster warm starts

Gaussian scale optimization can be expensive because every likelihood
evaluation has two costly pieces:

1. smooth the raster at the proposed `sigma`
2. solve the graph Laplacian to get resistance distances

For large rasters, you can ask `terradish()` to optimize first on one or more
aggregated rasters and then refine the result on the original surface:



The coarse stages rebuild the Gaussian smoother on each aggregated raster, but
they keep the full-resolution `sigma` bounds and unit conversion. That means a
coarse-stage `sigma` is still interpreted in the original map units. With
`exact_refine = TRUE`, the final model is not a coarse approximation; it is the
full-resolution fit initialized from the coarse result.

### Step 4: Inspect the fitted one-raster scale



The conductance coefficient tells us whether smoothed forest cover is
associated with higher or lower conductance. The scale parameter is already on
its natural scale, so we can summarize it directly:



**How to read the output.** The scale table includes its estimate, standard
error, and proximity to a fitting bound. Wald intervals from `confint()` are
limited to those bounds; a bound-limited interval does not establish an
interior optimum. Inspect the likelihood profile when scale is important:



The returned fit stores the profile interval. Increase grid resolution when
the profile is irregular. Failed profile fits stop the calculation rather
than being silently omitted. Boundary likelihood-ratio calibration remains
approximate; report a bound-limited interval as such.

`gaussian_scale_summary()` converts the fitted sigma values into:

- the native raster distance scale
- the internal optimization scale and its conversion factor
- raster cell widths
- one-dimensional Gaussian half-widths
- two-dimensional radial extents that contain a chosen amount of kernel mass



That table is often the quickest way to interpret the fitted smoothing scale.
For
example, `radial_95` is the radius that contains approximately 95% of the
untruncated Gaussian kernel mass, while `sigma_cells_x` and `sigma_cells_y` show how broad
the smoothing kernel is relative to the raster resolution. These ideal
Gaussian radii are descriptive; raster truncation and edge handling can
change the realized weights.

You can also visualize the fitted kernel directly. The `sigma` plot shows the
Gaussian weight curve over distance for each fitted scale parameter, with a
vertical dashed line at the fitted distance containing 90% of the
one-dimensional kernel mass and a shaded uncertainty band for that distance.
Each panel is also annotated with:

- the fitted `sigma`
- the fitted 90% kernel-mass distance
- the corresponding confidence interval bounds



If you already know how to convert your map units into a more interpretable
distance scale, you can relabel the plot directly. For example, if one map
unit corresponds to one meter, then multiplying by `0.001` reports distances
in kilometers. This conversion is only valid for a meter-based raster;
do not apply it to longitude/latitude degrees:



### Step 5: Compare against a standard fixed-raster fit

The scale-aware fit is easiest to interpret when it is compared directly to the
same model without an estimated `sigma`.





The two fits use the same response, sites, graph, and MLPE likelihood, so their
AIC values have a common basis. The scale-aware model's parameter count includes
the estimated smoothing scale. In a larger analysis, declare the candidate
scale and formula set before comparing it, and report the search that was
performed.

Assess whether the scale is identified away from its bounds, whether the
profile supports a useful range, and whether estimating it improves held-out
prediction. With only eight sites this illustration cannot provide a strong
spatial-transfer assessment. On a larger sampling design, use common folds
and a distinct conductance factory for each candidate:



Inspect failures and paired differences on common successful folds. A scale
that improves in-sample fit but not prediction needs cautious interpretation.

### Step 6: Summarize the one-raster fit



For this conductance model, the coefficient table includes both:

- the conductance coefficient, and
- `sigma`

So the scale parameter is treated just like any other model parameter in the
joint optimization.

Once the model is fitted, the usual conductance helper still works.

## Stage 2: Add a second raster only after sigma looks sensible

Once the one-raster scale parameter looks stable enough to trust, we can add a
second raster and inspect whether the scale estimates remain interpretable.







This is exactly the point where the staged workflow helps. If one or more
sigma estimates now collapse to the lower bound, or drift toward the upper
bound, that is useful information. It suggests one of several things:

- the added raster may not need smoothing at all
- the data may not support estimating multiple scales simultaneously
- the smaller one-raster scale was not as stable as it first appeared

Check convergence separately from boundary status. A converged boundary fit
can indicate limited scale information or preference for an extreme allowed
scale. A nonconverged fit requires numerical investigation before ecological
interpretation.

In the small `melip` subset used here, that is exactly the kind of pattern to
watch for: the one-raster `forestcover` scale can be inspected on its own
before adding `altitude`. After `altitude` is added, one or both fitted scale
parameters may move toward the lower bound, which is a cue to
proceed cautiously rather than immediately adding more landscape terms.

## Outer scale searches

`terradish_scale_optim()` remains available for an outer search, while joint
Gaussian fitting provides derivatives and joint scale inference directly.
Outer-search radii are in map units and the default outer kernel is Gaussian.
A Gaussian standard deviation, a uniform-disk radius, and other kernel
parameters represent different weighting functions even when their numeric
values match. Record the kernel, units, search bounds, and estimated scales.
The parameter count includes optimized scales; repeated searching is still
part of the declared model-selection procedure. Prefer projected rasters
when distances are to be interpreted in meters or kilometers.

## Important current limitations

This first version is intentionally focused. Right now it supports:

- additive formulas made of raw numeric raster names
- interactions among numeric raster names
- polynomial/arithmetic terms such as `I(forestcover^2)`
- one Gaussian scale parameter per selected raster
- joint optimization through analytic gradients and BFGS
- opt-in coarse-raster starts through `approximation = "coarse_raster"`

It does **not yet** support:

- factor-valued raster layers
- leverage / `partial_X` diagnostics

It does now support conditional-on-sigma marginal-association plotting, so you can
inspect conductance and response-scale curves after fitting, but the leverage
machinery is still intentionally disabled.

---

## Where to go with your analysis

If this workflow looks reasonable for your data, practical steps are:

1. Fit a small one-raster model first and inspect `sigma`.
2. Compare it to a standard fixed-raster `loglinear_conductance` fit.
3. Add additional rasters only after the one-raster scale parameter behaves
   sensibly.
4. Treat boundary-hitting sigma estimates as a model-selection signal, not just
   an optimization nuisance.
5. Reproject rasters before interpreting smoothing distances, because
   longitude/latitude rasters report sigma in degrees.

This staged approach makes it easier to assess whether the likelihood identifies
an interior Gaussian smoothing scale under the fitted model. An identified
smoothing scale is not, by itself, an identified movement or ecological-process
scale.

## What is terradish for?

`terradish` estimates conditional associations between raster covariates
(altitude, forest cover, roads, water bodies, etc.) and genetic distance
or covariance among sampled populations.

The core idea is **isolation by resistance (IBR)**: instead of assuming
that genetic distance simply tracks straight-line geographic distance
(isolation by distance, IBD), IBR represents the landscape as a
heterogeneous graph. Each cell has a fitted **conductance**, and
neighboring cells define weighted edges. Conductance is a relative model
quantity, not a direct observation of movement, migration, habitat
suitability, or permeability.

If we can map conductance across the landscape as a function of
measurable covariates (rasters), we can:

1.  Build a **conductance surface**: a weighted graph over the raster
    grid.
2.  Build the weighted graph Laplacian $L(\theta)$ and its generalized
    inverse $E(\theta)=L(\theta)^+$.
3.  Derive resistance distances from $E(\theta)$ when the measurement
    model requires them.
4.  Link $E(\theta)$ or its resistance distances to an observed genetic
    matrix with a selected measurement likelihood.
5.  Optimize the conductance and nuisance parameters by maximum
    likelihood.

Version 0.1.0 focuses on symmetric resistance models with log-linear,
fixed-degree spline, or jointly estimated Gaussian-scale conductance.
Environmental differences between sites can enter either MLPE or Wishart
measurement models. Directional, selected-pair, hierarchical, and other
experimental extensions are maintained on the `experimental` branch.

`terradish` automates these steps and provides gradients, standard
errors, model-comparison tools, and visualization helpers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## A conceptual road map

Before writing any code, it helps to see how the pieces fit together:



The **conductance model** maps raster values at each grid cell to a
conductance value. `loglinear_conductance` is the default: conductance =
exp(θ₁·altitude + θ₂·forestcover + ...). The parameters θ are what we
estimate.

The **measurement model** links the graph to the observed genetic summary.
For pairwise genetic distances, `mlpe` accounts for a shared-site component of
dependence; `leastsquares` assumes independent errors. For a suitably constructed
covariance matrix or its corresponding squared-distance matrix, the Wishart
models use a likelihood on site contrasts. The response construction and
inferential assumptions determine which family is appropriate. A Wishart fit
also requires an analyst-supplied effective degrees of freedom, `nu`.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Scale your rasters: an important step

Conductance in the loglinear model is exp(θ·x). If `x` (e.g. altitude in
meters) has a very large range, small changes in θ produce enormous
changes in conductance, making optimization numerically unstable.
**Always scale continuous covariates** before fitting.



By default, `scale_covariates()` subtracts each layer's mean and divides
by its standard deviation, giving every continuous covariate a mean of 0
and a standard deviation of 1. It also stores the original-scale center
and scale for each layer, which lets
`plot(fit, type = "marginal", data = surface)` label x-axes in the
original units automatically.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Build the conductance surface



`conductance_surface()` does three things:

1.  Reads the raster values at every non-NA grid cell.
2.  Identifies which cells correspond to the sampling locations.
3.  Builds a graph where adjacent cells (here, using all 8 neighbors
    including diagonals) are connected by edges whose weights will be
    determined by the conductance model.

`directions = 8` is the default; it allows diagonal
movement and is less sensitive to grid orientation than
`directions = 4`.



### Optional: reduce graph size for large rasters

The example raster is small enough that we can fit directly. For larger
landscapes, graph size becomes the main driver of runtime. If your
sampling locations occupy only part of the raster, you can crop the
raster to a buffered sampling extent before the graph is constructed:



The buffer is measured in the raster's map units. In a projected raster
this might be meters; in a longitude/latitude raster it is degrees.
Cropping is a safe speed tool only when the cropped area still contains
the landscape context needed to estimate resistance distances. A good
habit is to try a few buffer widths and confirm that the fitted
coefficients are stable.

Another speed option is a coarse-raster warm start. This fits the same
model on aggregated rasters first, then refines the estimate on the
full-resolution surface:



With `exact_refine = TRUE`, the final reported model is still evaluated
on the original graph. The coarse fits are only used to find better
starting values.

## Checking computational settings

For the small *Melipona* example the default settings run in seconds and
no tuning is needed. For larger landscapes (e.g., a high-resolution
raster with hundreds of thousands of graph vertices, or a spline
conductance model that expands to many parameters), the right optimizer
and solver can meaningfully reduce wall-clock time.

`terradish_assess_settings()` is an **advisory tool** for this. It:

1.  **Profiles the graph**: counts vertices, edges, focal sites, and
    conductance parameters, and reports those back without running any
    optimization.
2.  **Benchmarks the direct solver**: times several combinations of
    Cholesky factorization style and solve backend to find the fastest
    configuration for your specific graph.
3.  **Runs short optimizer probes**: executes two Newton steps for each
    of three optimizer/line-search combinations (Newton + Hager-Zhang,
    BFGS + Hager-Zhang, BFGS + Armijo) and reports which was fastest.
4.  **Optionally probes coarse-raster warm-starts**: checks whether
    fitting on an aggregated raster first produces an acceptable
    speedup.
5.  **Returns `$recommended`**: a named list of the best settings that
    can be passed directly to `terradish()`.

The function does **not** change any package defaults and does **not**
affect parameter estimation; only the computational path differs.



The printed output has three main parts:



- **Graph profile**: tells you how large your problem is. A vertex count
  above \~50 000 is where solver choice starts to matter; above \~500
  000 it is often the dominant cost.
- **Recommended settings**: the fastest combination found by the probes.
  `"(fastest acceptable probe: ...)"` shows which probe won and why.
- **Defaults comparison**: a one-line summary of whether the assessment
  recommends anything different from what `terradish()` would use by
  default. If it says `"Assessment matched terradish defaults"`, just
  call `terradish()` normally.

| Graph 10 000 to 100 000 vertices | Solver benchmark often helps |
| Graph \> 100 000 vertices | All three probes are worthwhile |
| Spline conductance (`df ≥ 4`) | Parameter expansion can shift optimizer recommendation from Newton to BFGS |
| Fitting many candidate models | Run assessment once, reuse `$recommended` for all fits |
| Coarse-raster approximation desired | Set `coarse_probe = TRUE` to check whether warm-start pays off |

### Troubleshooting direct-solver failures

If a fit stops with a `CHOLMOD` warning or a
`non-positive-definite reduced Laplacian` message, the direct sparse
Cholesky solver could not factorize the graph at the current conductance
values. This is usually a numerical or data-preparation problem rather
than a genetic-distance model result.

Immediate checks:

- Confirm the raster layers used by the conductance model are finite on
  the retained graph cells. Missing, infinite, or extremely large values
  can produce unusable conductances after the exponential transform.
- Scale continuous covariates with `scale_covariates()` and start
  conductance parameters near zero. Very large positive or negative
  starts can make some graph edges effectively infinite or zero.
- Re-run `terradish_assess_settings(..., verbose = TRUE)` to inspect
  graph size, direct-solver settings, and the optimizer path before
  launching a long fit.
- While diagnosing the surface, try `solver = "auto"` or
  `solver = "amg"`. These paths do not fix invalid conductance values,
  but they can help separate a direct Cholesky factorization issue from
  a broader model setup problem.

We will start with the simplest case: are altitude and forest cover
conditionally associated with genetic differentiation through the fitted
conductance model?



The formula works like a standard R formula:

- The **left-hand side** is the matrix of observed genetic distances.

  conductance.

`leastsquares` is the quickest measurement model. It treats the pairwise
genetic distances as independent observations regressed on resistance
distances. That is not strictly correct (measurements sharing a site are
correlated), but it gives a useful exploratory or diagnostic fit.



**How to read the output:**

- **theta**: the conductance parameters. A positive θ for a covariate
  means that higher values are associated with higher fitted relative
  conductance, conditional on other terms and the graph. A negative θ
  means lower fitted relative conductance. Neither sign is a direct
  estimate of movement or a causal landscape effect.
- **phi**: nuisance parameters of the measurement model. For MLPE, these
  are the intercept, slope, log precision, and an unconstrained
  correlation parameter. The actual residual precision is `exp(tau)`,
  and the actual shared-site correlation is `plogis(rho) / 2`. These
  parameters are profiled out automatically and generally are not the
  primary inferential target.
- The standard errors and z-statistics help gauge parameter uncertainty.

## Upgrade to the MLPE measurement model

Pairwise genetic distances are not independent: the distance between
sites A-B and A-C both involve site A. `mlpe` ("maximum likelihood
population effects") accounts for this correlation structure and is the
recommended choice for inference.



Compare the point estimates and standard errors between `fit_ls` and
`fit_mlpe`. They are often similar, but MLPE standard errors better
reflect the shared-site dependence represented by the model.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Choosing a measurement model

`terradish` provides four measurement models. The right choice depends
on the form of your genetic data and how you want to handle the
non-independence of pairwise observations.

| Model | Input `S` | Requires `nu` | Correlation structure |
|--------------------|------------------|-------------|---------------------|
| `leastsquares` | distance matrix | no | none (independent errors) |
| `mlpe` | distance matrix | no | MLPE shared-site correction |
| `generalized_wishart` | admissible squared-distance matrix | yes | generalized Wishart |
| `wishart_covariance` | **covariance** matrix | yes | Wishart |

`leastsquares` is the fastest and simplest. It is a reasonable first
pass, but its standard errors can be too small because it ignores
shared-site correlation.

`mlpe` models a shared-site component of dependence because pairs (A-B) and
(A-C) both involve site A. It uses maximum likelihood, not restricted maximum
likelihood. This correction does not guarantee calibrated uncertainty for every
population process. Treat `leastsquares` as a simple comparator rather than a
general solution to dependence.

`generalized_wishart` requires a squared-distance response coherently related to
a positive-semidefinite covariance matrix. Use `check_distance_response()` on
the exact response. Passing that check establishes compatible geometry, not a
valid sampling model. In particular, F~ST~ is a ratio estimator; it does not
acquire a Wishart sampling distribution merely by passing the geometry check.
For a generic genetic dissimilarity, consider MLPE and assess its assumptions.

`wishart_covariance` accepts the covariance directly, for example from
`cov_from_biallelic()`. Both Wishart interfaces evaluate the same contrast
likelihood on the site-contrast subspace when their inputs are related by
`dist_from_cov()`. Centering the covariance does not discard information used
by that likelihood. The previous full-rank covariance formulation is removed.

### What nu means

`nu` is a positive effective degrees-of-freedom value supplied by the analyst.
The fit does not estimate it. Raw SNP counts, locus counts, and allele contrasts
are not automatically effective independent observations: linkage and shared
population history can reduce the information they contain. A block jackknife
can diagnose marker dependence, but it does not capture every source of shared
history and should be treated as an upper-bound guide to precision.

For a fixed response and model, increasing `nu` leaves the theoretical optimum
unchanged while reducing interior standard errors in proportion to
`1 / sqrt(nu)`. It also magnifies likelihood differences and can favor flexible
models. State the chosen value and its rationale, compare plausible values
with `terradish_rescale_nu()`, and use held-out prediction to assess conductance
complexity. See `vignette("wishart-covariance", package = "terradish")` and
`vignette("model-comparison", package = "terradish")`.

### Fitting with `generalized_wishart`



**How to read the response check:** `admissible = TRUE` means the
centered Gram matrix is positive semidefinite within the documented
numerical tolerance. `relative_min_eigenvalue` reports the smallest
eigenvalue relative to the largest; a tiny negative value can reflect
rounding. `n_negative` counts only violations that exceed the tolerance.
This diagnostic establishes mathematical compatibility with the
likelihood, not biological suitability of the chosen genetic summary.

The nuisance parameters for `generalized_wishart` are `tau` (the weight
on the graph covariance kernel) and `sigma` (a log-scale identity
component that captures diagonal variation not represented by that
kernel). `sigma` is not a Gaussian smoothing scale or a direct estimate
of genetic drift. A `tau` estimate near zero indicates no detectable
graph-kernel contribution under this response and model.

You can compare models that use the **same response, sites, graph
domain, likelihood family, and pair subset** with `aic_table()`; Wishart
comparisons must also use the same `nu`. `leastsquares` and `mlpe` both
model pairwise distances with a Gaussian likelihood, so they can be
compared when those conditions hold. `generalized_wishart` evaluates a
Wishart likelihood on the same distance matrix, which has a different
functional form and different normalizing constants; its AIC values are
not directly comparable to those of `leastsquares` or `mlpe`. Use
`aic_table()` to rank models within a family (e.g., all `mlpe` fits
differing in their conductance formula, or all `generalized_wishart`
fits differing in their conductance formula).

### Uncertainty and convergence



**How to read the output.** `vcov()` gives the covariance of the conductance
coefficient estimates; `confint()` reports intervals on their fitted scale.
For a log-linear coefficient, exponentiate both interval endpoints to obtain
an interval for the relative conductance ratio. These are conditional model
intervals, not direct intervals for migration or movement rates. At a
no-structure boundary, conductance coefficients are unidentified and their
inference is unavailable.

### Convergence and starting values

Check convergence before interpreting estimates. Code 0 requires either a
projected gradient below `ctol`, or an objective change below `ftol` together
with a projected gradient below `sqrt(ctol)`. The projected gradient omits
components pointing outside active parameter bounds. Code 1 means the
iteration limit was reached; code 2 means a stall or failed line search. A
small likelihood change alone is insufficient. Also inspect the nested
nuisance fit's convergence code.

If a user-supplied starting point reaches a no-structure boundary, the fitter
tries the default start and retains the better likelihood. The convergence
record reports whether that restart occurred. For difficult fits, compare
scientifically plausible starting values and inspect the final likelihoods,
gradients, coefficients, and active bounds. Increasing the iteration limit can
help a progressing fit; weakening tolerances does not resolve a stalled one.
Numerical convergence alone does not establish model adequacy.

## Visualize the fit

### Observed vs. fitted genetic distances

The `plot()` S3 method with `type = "fit"` produces an

### Fitted conductance surface

`type = "surface"` renders the point-estimate conductance surface
alongside its lower and upper confidence interval bounds in a single
three-panel figure. All panels share the same color scale so relative
differences are easy to read:



Brighter cells have higher fitted relative conductance. Panels where the
lower and upper bounds are close together indicate narrower conditional
uncertainty under the fitted model; they do not directly measure
movement or permeability.

### Marginal associations with predicted genetic distance (default)

The default plot (`type = "marginal_response"`) shows how each raster
covariate is associated with the **predicted genetic distance** (the
response variable itself) rather than the intermediate conductance
scale. Because genetic distance is the observed response that the model
fits, this is usually the most directly interpretable view.



**How to read these plots**

Each panel sweeps one covariate across its observed range while all
other covariates are kept at their *observed* values at every grid cell
(not at a single mean value). The curve is therefore a weighted average
over the actual landscape, which matters most when the associations of
different covariates interact.

The **central curve** is the expected genetic distance at the given
covariate value, marginalized over the rest of the landscape.

The **shaded predictive band** combines three sources of uncertainty:

1.  *Theta (conductance parameter) uncertainty*: imprecision in the
    estimated conductance parameters θ, propagated through the
    resistance-distance calculation via the delta method.
2.  *Phi (measurement-model parameter) uncertainty*: imprecision in the
    nuisance parameters (intercept α, IBR slope β, log precision τ, and
    the unconstrained correlation parameter ρ), again via the delta
    method. The corresponding shared-site correlation is
    `plogis(rho) / 2`.
3.  *Residual variation*: the irreducible scatter in pairwise genetic
    distances around the fitted mean, with variance `exp(-tau)`.

A **rising curve** means that higher covariate values are associated
with higher predicted genetic distance under the fitted model. A
**declining curve** means the conditional predicted association is
negative. These plots do not establish a causal permeability or
connectivity mechanism.

> **Note:** Quadratic terms (`I(x^2)`) and interactions (`x * z`) in the
> formula are handled automatically. For an interaction, each panel
> holds the other covariate at its observed values across all grid
> cells, so the curve represents the average fitted association of the
> focal covariate over the joint distribution of the landscape.

### Marginal associations on the conductance scale

If you prefer to see the fitted association on the underlying
conductance surface (without residual variation in the band, and on the
conductance rather than genetic distance scale), use
`type = "marginal"`:



Here the band is a **confidence interval** (delta-method, 95% pointwise)
on the mean conductance, not a predictive interval; it reflects only
parameter uncertainty in θ, not residual variation. A rising curve means
higher covariate values are associated with higher fitted conductance; a
declining curve means the fitted conditional association is negative.

### Support-constrained prediction and plotting

If a covariate has sparse focal support at one tail, marginal curves and
conductance maps can be dominated by extrapolation. `terradish` now
supports focal-support clamping directly in both `plot()` and
`conductance()`:



Focal support means the covariate values in the raster cells containing
sampled sites. The default `support_probs = c(0, 1)` uses their minimum and
maximum; the example uses their 1st and 99th percentiles. Values outside those
limits are set to the nearest limit before conductance is evaluated. For
splines, evaluation retains the fitted knots and basis definition.

Use `support = "none"` (default) for full-domain evaluation. Clamping with
`support = "focal"` changes the displayed or predicted surface; it does not
refit the likelihood, change the fitted graph, or remove uncertainty about
poorly sampled environments. Report the limits and compare both maps.
Predictions on new rasters must retain training transformations; use
`scale_covariates(new_covariates, reference = covariates)` before building the
new surface rather than scaling each domain independently.

## What the joint model estimates

**Isolation by resistance (IBR)** associates genetic structure with resistance
through a landscape graph. **Isolation by environment (IBE)** associates genetic
structure with environmental differences between sampled sites. Fitting both
estimates each association conditional on the other predictors. It does not
allocate genetic variation to distinct causal mechanisms.

This vignette builds one pairwise design containing geographic distance and
scaled environmental differences, then uses it in both measurement families.
A small synthetic example keeps the complete workflow runnable. See
`vignette("getting-started", package = "terradish")` for preparing real rasters
and `vignette("wishart-covariance", package = "terradish")` for genotype summaries.

The two families connect the same pairwise differences to their responses:

| Component | MLPE distance regression | Wishart contrast covariance |
|----|----|----|
| Landscape | Resistance `R(theta)` with slope `beta` | Graph covariance `E(theta)` with weight `tau` |
| Pairwise difference `D_k` | Additive mean term `gamma_k * D_k` | Centered kernel `K_k = -H D_k H / 2` with weight `lambda_k` |
| Environmental constraint | `gamma_k` can have either sign | `lambda_k >= 0` |
| Remaining variation | Intercept, residual precision, shared-site correlation | Common covariance nugget |
| Effective information | Determined by the fitted MLPE likelihood | Analyst supplies `nu` |

Here `H` centers sites. For the corresponding covariance-derived distances,
the Wishart model implies the additive mean
`tau * R + sum(lambda_k * D_k) + 2 * exp(sigma)`. This makes the distance
interpretation comparable to MLPE, although the likelihoods and constraints
differ. The default `absdiff` kernel is not an outer product of site values;
the `sqdiff` transform gives that special case.

## 1. Build a landscape and one pairwise design



**How to read the output.** Each row is an unordered site pair in R's
lower-triangle order. The climate column is the absolute difference after
standardizing site values; one unit is one site-level standard deviation of
difference. Geographic distance retains the coordinate units, meters here.
`pairwise_covariates()` does not scale either input again. In real studies,
use a projection suitable for the study region and retain the same site order
in the response, graph, and pairwise design.

The supported transforms are `absdiff`, `sqdiff`, `euclidean`, and `manhattan`.
Choose the transform before fitting and retain it across families. A Wishart
kernel must be positive semidefinite in site contrasts; the constructor rejects
incompatible pairwise geometry rather than silently correcting it.



These are measurement-model factories. Their coefficients are estimated when
the resistance model is fitted. Both retain the original pairwise design so
site subsets do not independently restandardize environmental values.

## 2. Create covariance and matching distance responses

For an interpretable comparison between families, use the same covariance
information represented in two forms. This example draws from a declared
Wishart model with landscape, climate, and geographic components. It does
not imply that a generic genetic distance or F~ST~ follows that distribution.



The diagnostic checks distance geometry. Here the generating model also
supplies a sampling justification. For real data, assess that justification
separately. `nu_demo` is an illustrative simulation setting, not a recommendation
to use the number of SNPs as effective degrees of freedom.

## 3. Fit the candidate models within each family

The uniform surface still implies resistance among sites. Adding pairwise
environmental terms to it is therefore **IBD + IBE (uniform surface)**, not
an IBE-only model. Geographic distance is an additional explicit measurement
predictor; uniform-graph resistance is generally not Euclidean distance.



Interpret code 0 as numerical convergence under the stopping criteria.
Inspect nonzero codes, gradients, inner nuisance convergence, and active
bounds before comparing fits. A no-resistance boundary makes the conductance
surface unidentified; a zero environmental weight alone does not.

## 4. Read conditional conductance and environmental associations



**How to read the output.** A positive `x` coefficient means greater relative
conductance as `x` increases, conditional on the included pairwise terms.
The estimated coefficient can change after adding those terms because the
model can represent the same spatial pattern in more than one way. Neither
surface is a unique causal map of landscape effects.

In MLPE, an environmental `gamma` gives the conditional change in genetic
distance per unit of pairwise difference. It is unconstrained, so a negative
estimate is possible. In Wishart, `lambda` is a nonnegative kernel weight.
An association in the opposite direction is represented at the zero bound.
The Wishart summary marks that boundary and gives an approximate one-sided
upper limit rather than a symmetric interval.



This reports the MLPE response change over the stored 10th-to-90th-percentile
pairwise contrast. The nuisance inference includes uncertainty in the fitted
conductance parameters. It remains conditional on the selected model and
measurement assumptions.

### Compare IBE:IBR ratios



The ratio is `gamma / beta` for MLPE and `lambda / tau` for Wishart. It is
the resistance-distance equivalent of one unit of the pairwise difference.
These ratios share an interpretation because the response is the same
covariance-derived distance and both families use the same pairwise transform
and scaling. Their absolute coefficients are not comparable across likelihoods.
Each fit also estimates its own conductance surface, so its resistance scale
can differ. The ratio is undefined when the resistance coefficient is zero.
The geographic row has the same equivalent-distance interpretation but is
an explicit IBD term, not an environmental mechanism.

An environmental association can arise from local adaptation, historical
structure, correlated geography, sampling, or another omitted predictor.
The fitted term does not distinguish these mechanisms. A precise ratio alone
does not establish causal IBE.

## 5. Compare predictive support and sensitivity

Information criteria are computed separately within each likelihood family:



Lower AIC balances fit and parameter count within the declared candidate set.
Wishart AIC can over-select environmental kernels when `nu` overstates effective
information. AICc does not repair that problem. Cross-family AIC comparisons
have no common likelihood basis.

### Nested tests and boundaries



Adding unconstrained MLPE slopes uses the ordinary chi-square reference under
its asymptotic assumptions. Adding only nonnegative Wishart kernel weights
uses a chi-bar-square reference with binomial weights. This reference is exact
for information-orthogonal kernels and approximate otherwise. The climatic
and geographic kernels here need not be orthogonal. Wishart test magnitudes
also depend on supplied `nu`; treat them as descriptive at that value.
The uniform-surface IBE model and the IBR model are not nested in each other.



Rescaling retains coefficient estimates and broadens uncertainty when `nu`
decreases. Rescale every candidate to the same value for a model comparison.
This is a sensitivity calculation, not estimation of effective information.

### Fixed-nuisance cross-validation



All nuisance coefficients, including pairwise terms, are learned from training
sites and held fixed during scoring. Compare candidates within each family,
using paired differences on common successful folds. Reprofiling on test sites
would instead assess performance after local recalibration. For spatial
transfer, use defensible spatial blocks and more sites than this teaching
example. Repeated folds expose partition sensitivity without creating new
independent observations.

## 6. Visualize the joint model



Agreement describes the fitted response summary. It does not show that the
two spatial predictors identify distinct processes.



The map and its intervals concern the conductance component after including
pairwise terms. Compare it with the fit omitting those terms before attributing
the spatial pattern to landscape `x`.



For weakly sampled tails, compare unclamped predictions with
`support = "focal"`. Clamping uses focal-cell covariate limits and changes
prediction, not the fitted likelihood or the evidence for a mechanism.

thousands to millions of grid cells. If you have not read

## When a landscape is "large"

`terradish` builds a weighted graph over the raster grid and solves a linear
system on that graph to get resistance distances among focal sites. The cost of
that solve grows with the number of grid cells, `N`. Two things change as `N`
grows into the hundreds of thousands and beyond:

1. The linear **solver** that computes resistance distances can run out of
   memory. Picking the right one (Section 3) is what keeps a fit feasible.
2. The **inference** machinery (standard errors and confidence intervals) is
   unchanged in form, but larger analyses can include more focal sites. Wishart
   precision also depends on the supplied effective degrees of freedom, `nu`
   (Section 4).

"Large" here means that graph factorization or repeated derivative solves
dominate the analysis. There is no universal cell-count limit: missing cells,
graph connectivity, and conductance contrast can change the practical limit.

---

## Choosing a solver

Both `terradish()` (the fitting wrapper) and `terradish_algorithm()` (one
likelihood and gradient evaluation) take a `solver` argument. The solver only
changes **how** the resistance-distance linear system is solved; it does not
change the statistical model. Iterative solvers should agree with the direct
solver within their numerical tolerances, but estimates need not be bitwise
identical.



The choices are:

- **`direct`** (default) uses sparse Cholesky factorization. It is a useful
  reference for checking iterative results. The factor can contain many more
  nonzero entries than the graph, so its memory requirements can grow rapidly.
- **`amg`** uses algebraic multigrid as an iterative solver. It can reduce memory
  demands on large grids, but convergence depends on conductance contrast and
  graph structure. Strong barriers can require more iterations or tighter
  solver settings. Check agreement with a direct solve on a manageable case.
- **`auto`** uses `direct` below 1,500,000 graph vertices and `amg` at or above
  that threshold with the default controls. The number of focal sites does not
  override the large-graph switch. `solver_control$auto_amg_min_vertices`
  changes the threshold; the legacy `auto_direct_max_rhs` is ignored.

Use `terradish_assess_settings()` for a local speed and accuracy probe. Its
recommendation concerns the tested settings and graph, not the scientific
adequacy of the measurement model. Record the resolved solver and compare
coefficient estimates and likelihoods when changing solver tolerances.

This applies to every symmetric resistance model: any `conductance_model`
(`loglinear_conductance`, `smooth_loglinear_conductance`, the Gaussian
scale-aware model) paired with any measurement model (`mlpe`,
`leastsquares`, `generalized_wishart`, and `wishart_covariance`). The solver sits underneath
all of them.

---

## Inference at scale

Scaling the solver lets you fit larger graph domains and sampling designs. More
informative designs can tighten inference, but cell count alone does not.
Several computational choices matter here.

## Reusing Windows workers

Worker reuse is **experimental**. On Windows, `cores > 1` requests PSOCK
workers for supported Hessian and partial-derivative calculations. It does
not parallelize every step of a fit. The pool is reused during optimization
and stopped before return; fitted objects do not retain live workers.
AMG and the cached CHOLMOD backend ignore this setting. On small graphs,
worker startup and data transfer can make multiple cores slower than one.
Benchmark `cores = 1` first, then compare the elapsed time and numerical
results with the requested worker count.

Use one level of parallelism at a time. In particular, fixed-domain
cross-validation evaluates folds serially and passes `cores` to each fit. This
avoids creating an outer fold cluster whose workers each create another inner
cluster.

## Controlling the nuisance profile

Each conductance-parameter trial profiles the nuisance parameters of the
measurement model. Smooth conductance models paired with
`generalized_wishart` can make that two-level optimization costly because each
outer step may require many inner Newton steps. The inner optimizer already
uses the previous nuisance solution as a warm start. Use
`measurement_control` to inspect or tune it separately from `control`:



The returned `convergence` code and `iters` count describe the final nuisance
profile. A value of 0 indicates convergence; 1 means the profile reached
`maxit`; 2 indicates a line-search failure. Do not loosen tolerances simply to
remove a warning. First inspect coefficient stability, gradient size, the
likelihood, and sensitivity to tighter settings. The control separates a
computational decision from the scientific model, but overly loose profiling
can still change the fitted likelihood surface.

## Curvature for standard errors

`terradish()` and `terradish_algorithm()` take `curvature = c("exact",
"gauss_newton")`. The objective and gradient are the same; the options differ
in the Hessian approximation used for optimization and standard errors. Fits
should be compared for numerical agreement rather than assumed identical.



- **`exact`** uses the full observed Hessian.
- **`gauss_newton`** drops the two residual-weighted second-derivative terms,
  leaving the **Gauss-Newton information** curvature. It needs only the
  first derivatives of conductance and is positive semidefinite when the
  retained measurement-model curvature is positive semidefinite. It flows
  unchanged into `vcov()`, `summary()`, and `confint()`, so the standard errors
  become asymptotic, information-based errors. It approaches the exact Hessian
  when the omitted residual-weighted terms are negligible; equality is not
  guaranteed merely because a fit has converged.

**How to choose.** Both are validated for `leastsquares` and the
`generalized_wishart` measurement model. Use the `exact` default for routine
work. Reach for `gauss_newton` when the optimizer struggles with an indefinite
Hessian far from the optimum, or when you want strictly information-based
standard errors. At a well-behaved fit they may agree closely. Compare them as
a sensitivity check when curvature choice materially changes uncertainty or
convergence.

The focused benchmark in `inst/benchmarks/benchmark-curvature-large-landscape.R`
makes this comparison reproducible. It builds a synthetic heterogeneous raster,
simulates a `generalized_wishart` squared-distance response from known conductance
parameters, then fits the same model with `curvature = "exact"` and
`curvature = "gauss_newton"`.



The default case is deliberately small enough to run during review. To repeat
the same comparison in the large-N solver regime, copy `CONFIG`, increase
`side`, and set `solver = "amg"` or `solver = "auto"` before calling
`run_curvature_large_landscape_benchmark()`.

**How to read the benchmark.** Compare coefficients and likelihoods first,
then standard errors, Hessian eigenvalues, and elapsed time. Agreement in
point estimates does not guarantee agreement in curvature. A large difference
in uncertainty warrants investigation of residuals, conditioning, and model
misspecification before choosing the faster option.

## Information and effective degrees of freedom

For Wishart fits, `nu` is a positive effective degrees-of-freedom value supplied
by the analyst. It is not estimated from the response and is not automatically
the marker count. Linkage and shared population history can reduce the
information represented by many markers. More graph cells alone do not supply
more independent genetic information.

Holding the response and model fixed, changing `nu` leaves the likelihood
optimum unchanged in theory and changes interior standard errors in proportion
to `1 / sqrt(nu)`. Use `terradish_rescale_nu()` to inspect that sensitivity
without refitting. Boundary inference needs separate care. Choose conductance
complexity using held-out prediction and report inference across plausible
`nu` values; see `vignette("model-comparison", package = "terradish")`.

## Cropping and coarse warm starts

Cropping removes routes outside the retained domain and can increase resistance
between sites even when every site remains inside the crop. The supplied
release audit found increases of 15–23% with a two-cell buffer and 1–2% with a
ten-cell buffer. Those are results for that landscape, not universal buffer
recommendations. Compare progressively wider buffers while retaining the same
response, sites, formula, and covariate scaling.



**How to read the output.** Compare estimates and intervals across buffers,
and inspect fitted resistance distances with `terradish_distance()`. Continue
expanding the domain if the substantive conclusion changes. The small-buffer
message is a diagnostic prompt, not proof that a larger buffer is adequate.

Coarse rasters can provide starting values, but the final fit must be refined
on the original graph. Coarsening changes both routes and covariate summaries.
The supported coarse-raster workflow therefore requires exact refinement:



Inspect convergence after the final refinement. A converged coarse fit does
not establish convergence on the original graph. `terradish_grid()` accepts
only `approximation = "none"`; coarse-only likelihoods are not supported
for ranking models.

## Storing large fitted objects

Fitted conductance and measurement-model closures can retain their construction
environments, including large graph objects. That is useful for prediction but
can make `saveRDS()` much larger than the visible coefficient table suggests.
Use `slim_terradish()` when the durable artifact is intended for inference
summaries rather than later prediction:



The default removes `submodels` and `leverage`. Coefficients, likelihood,
Hessian-based summaries, fitted values, diagnostics, and permutation
simulation remain available. Conductance prediction and most plot types do not,
because they must reevaluate the removed closures. Slimming is irreversible.
Keep the original formula, graph, response, and an unslimmed fit whenever
prediction or model reconstruction may be needed. Add `"response"` to `drop`
only when the response itself must also be excluded from the archive; doing so
also removes the strongest stored check for later model comparison.

## Overview

Once you can fit a `terradish` model (see
`vignette("getting-started", package = "terradish")`), the natural next
questions are:

1.  Does forest cover have conditional likelihood support after
    accounting for the other predictors, or could the apparent
    association be noise? (**hypothesis testing**)
2.  Is a model with both altitude and forest cover better than one with
    only altitude? (**model selection**)
3.  How well does the model predict genetic structure at sites not used
    for fitting? (**cross-validation**)

This vignette walks through all three.

## Hypothesis testing with likelihood ratio tests

A **likelihood ratio test (LRT)** asks whether adding a covariate
improves the model fit enough to reject the reduced model under its
assumptions. The test statistic is:

> 2 × (log-likelihood of larger model − log-likelihood of smaller model)

Under the null hypothesis that the extra parameter does not help, this
statistic follows asymptotically a χ² distribution with degrees of
freedom equal to the number of extra parameters. This approximation
improves as sample size (number of focal sites) increases.

`terradish` provides `anova()` for this:



A small p-value means that removing altitude worsens the likelihood more
than expected under the reduced model and its asymptotic reference
distribution. This result supports a conditional association between
altitude and genetic structure through the fitted conductance surface.
It does not by itself establish a causal effect, movement mechanism, or
absolute permeability.

### Testing against the null model of isolation by distance (IBD)

The formula `~ 1` fits a null conductance surface where all cells have
equal conductance. Resistance still combines all routes through the graph;
it is neither shortest-path distance nor necessarily proportional to Euclidean
distance. This uniform-surface model is the graph-based IBD baseline:



### Testing for an interaction

An interaction term asks whether the fitted association between altitude
and conductance depends on forest cover (or vice versa):



> **Tip:** Always compare nested models (smaller vs. larger). The model
> with fewer parameters must be a special case of the model with more
> parameters. `anova()` rejects different measurement models and obvious
> formula non-nesting, but the analyst must still verify substantive
> nesting and whether boundary parameters invalidate the usual
> chi-squared reference distribution. The response, site order, graph,
> pairwise design, basis definition, and supplied Wishart `nu` must agree.
> A linear term is not automatically nested in an arbitrary spline basis, and
> a fitted Gaussian scale changes the model dimension. For Wishart fits,
> the LRT magnitude depends on `nu`; interpret it descriptively at the declared
> value and report sensitivity rather than treating nominal marker counts as
> calibrated replication.

## Information criteria: AIC, AICc, and BIC

Likelihood ratio tests require nested models. When comparing non-nested
models (e.g. altitude-only vs. forest-cover-only), use an information
criterion or held-out prediction instead.



`aic_table()` ranks fitted `terradish` models only when they use the
same response, focal sites, graph domain, measurement-model family, and
pair subset. Wishart fits must also use the same supplied `nu`.
Cross-family comparisons, such as MLPE versus Wishart, do not have a
common likelihood basis and are rejected.



**How to read the table:**

- **logLik**: larger is better (less negative = higher likelihood).
- **df**: number of estimated parameters; more complex models have
  higher df.
- **AIC**: balances fit and complexity. Smaller is better.
- **delta**: difference from the best model's AIC. Values below about 2
  are a common heuristic for similar support within the declared
  candidate set, not proof that the models are equivalent.

### Effective information and over-selection

Wishart AIC can select unnecessary conductance terms when nominal `nu`
overstates effective information. AICc does not repair an incorrectly scaled
likelihood. Use the same predeclared candidates in cross-validation and
compare inference over plausible effective degrees of freedom:



**How to read the output.** Rescaling changes uncertainty and likelihood-based
comparisons without changing the fitted coefficients. If conclusions depend
on a high `nu`, report that dependence. Rescale every candidate to the same
value before comparing their information criteria.

### When to use AICc

AIC can over-fit when the effective sample size is small relative to the
number of parameters. `terradish` provides an AICc calculation that uses
the number of focal sampling sites as `n`:



The focal-site convention recognizes that pairwise genetic observations
are dependent, but it is still an approximation. Report the convention
and use the result as one part of the evidence. A common rule of thumb
is to prefer AICc when `n / K < 40`, where `K` includes all estimated
parameters. Predeclare and report the candidate model set and any search
over Gaussian smoothing scale; an optimized scale is an estimated
parameter.

### BIC for stricter parsimony

BIC penalizes complexity more heavily than AIC and tends to favor
simpler models. The implementation uses the number of pairwise
observations (`n = n_sites * (n_sites - 1) / 2`) as the BIC sample size.
AICc instead uses the number of focal sites. Which count is the most
appropriate `n` for landscape-genetic data is debated; treat BIC values
as one input among several rather than a single definitive arbiter of
model choice:

## Cross-validation

### Spatially blocked, fixed-domain cross-validation

Random splits ask whether a model predicts sites withheld throughout the
sampled landscape. They do not directly ask whether the fitted
association transfers across space. For that question, construct spatial
folds and use `terradish_cv_folds()`.

The fixed-domain method builds the graph once, then changes only which focal sites
contribute to the training and test responses. Unsampled raster cells,
graph edges, and raster covariates remain available in every fold. This
matches the prediction target of transferring to held-out sites within
the observed landscape.

Use ecological blocks when the study design supplies them. Watersheds,
barrier units, or sampling campaigns can be more defensible than an
automated partition. When such blocks are unavailable,
`terradish_folds()` provides a reproducible k-means partition of
unscaled projected coordinates:



With `nuisance = "fixed"`, all measurement-model coefficients are learned
from training sites and used unchanged to score held-out sites. This tests
prediction from the trained model. A separately fitted uniform-conductance
baseline provides `loglik_gain`; positive gain favors the trained landscape
surface over that baseline on the same held-out response.

The default `nuisance = "reprofile"` instead optimizes nuisance coefficients
using each test response, separately for the trained surface and baseline.
That asks whether the learned surface retains useful structure after local
recalibration. It is a different prediction target. Declare the mode and do
not compare scores across modes.

### Different models and repeated folds

Each formula can have its own conductance or measurement-model factory. For
example, compare a linear forest-cover term with a fixed-degree spline using
the same response and folds:



**How to read the output.** Inspect convergence and score failures first.
Rankings use folds on which every candidate succeeded. `mean_difference`
and `se_difference` describe paired score differences against the best
candidate on those common folds; `within_one_se` flags a difference no larger
than its estimated standard error. Prefer a simpler model when its predictive
performance is indistinguishable under the declared design. Overlapping
training sets and repeated partitions make these standard errors descriptive,
not independent-replicate hypothesis tests. Repeats expose split sensitivity;
they do not create new independent sites.

Checkpoint files retain completed fits and scores. Resume only the identical
response, graph, formulas, model settings, and folds; changed inputs are
rejected. Keep failed baseline scores separate from failed trained-model scores:
a valid held-out score can exist even when its baseline gain is unavailable.

The comparison boundary remains strict. Raw held-out log-likelihood and
`loglik_gain` are meaningful only when candidates use the same response
representation, measurement likelihood, folds, graph domain, and Wishart
degrees of freedom. A gain from MLPE is not on a common scale with a
gain from the generalized-Wishart or covariance-Wishart likelihood.
Spatial cross-validation strengthens evidence for transfer under the
declared blocking design. It does not establish a causal landscape
effect or prediction beyond the observed landscape domain.

## Combining the evidence

A complete analysis typically uses all three approaches together:

| Tool | Answers | When to use |
|----|----|----|
| `anova()` | Does a larger model have incremental likelihood support? | Nested models within one measurement family |
| `aic_table()` | Which declared candidate has the strongest relative support? | Models satisfying the full comparison contract |
| `terradish_cv_folds()` | Which candidate transfers best across declared spatial blocks? | Fixed-domain prediction under identical folds and one likelihood family |
| `mlpe_response_change()` | How large is an MLPE pairwise-covariate association on the response scale? | IBE terms fitted with `mlpe_covariates()` |

Use cross-validation to assess conductance terms and shape, then use `nu`
sensitivity to describe the uncertainty of Wishart inference. Treat an LRT at
a fixed `nu` as conditional, descriptive evidence. Information criteria add
a view of in-sample fit and complexity, but agreement among these summaries
does not validate the underlying effective sample size. None establishes
causality, observed movement, absolute permeability, or unique attribution to
a landscape mechanism.

## Why simulate before you sample?

Landscape-genetic studies are expensive: every additional sampling site means
field time and cost. Before
committing to a design it is worth asking, in advance:

- **How many sampling sites** do I need to detect the conditional conductance
  association specified in my scenario?
- **How much approximately independent marker information** (represented by
  the supplied `nu`) do I need?
- **Where should the sites go**: spread evenly across the landscape, or placed
  some other way?
- If I run the analysis, **how often will I recover the generating parameters
  under this model**, and how often will sampling variation obscure them?

`terradish` answers these questions by simulation. Because the Wishart
covariance model has a tractable generative form, you can draw synthetic
covariance matrices from a *known* conductance surface, refit the model, and
measure how reliably the generating parameters are recovered, all without any forward-time
genetic simulation. This vignette shows how.

This is a **design tool**, not a replacement for individual-based or
coalescent simulation. It assumes the fitted model is correctly specified and
asks: given that model and this set of scenarios, how much data do I need? The
answer is conditional on the graph, parameters, candidate set, likelihood,
optimizer, and sampling strategies evaluated here.

---

## The generative model

A covariance-based `terradish` fit (see
`vignette("wishart-covariance", package = "terradish")`) assumes the observed
genetic covariance matrix `S` is a noisy realization of a structured
covariance:

$$\Sigma = \tau \, E(\theta) + \sigma\, I, \qquad
  \nu\, S \sim \mathrm{Wishart}(\nu, \Sigma)$$

where:

- $E(\theta)$ is the **conductance-implied covariance**: the generalized
  inverse of the graph Laplacian at conductance parameters $\theta$. This is
  the part that carries the landscape signal.
- $\tau \ge 0$ scales that signal.
- $\sigma \ge 0$ is a diagonal residual-variance term for covariance variation
  not represented by $E(\theta)$. It is not a direct estimate of drift,
  population size, or a Gaussian smoothing scale.
- $\nu$ is the supplied **effective Wishart degrees of freedom**, not a fitted
  parameter. It can be interpreted as the effective number of approximately
  independent marker contributions. Large $\nu$ gives draws tightly clustered
  around $\Sigma$; small $\nu$ gives noisier draws.

`simulate_covariance_response()` draws from exactly this model, and
`covariance_response_power()` automates the simulate-refit-summarize loop across
many designs. Fitted `wishart_covariance()` models store the nugget internally
on the log scale, but both simulation helpers accept $\sigma$ as the
nonnegative variance added to the diagonal.

For a fixed landscape template, $\tau / \sigma$ is a convenient
**signal-to-nugget ratio**. Larger values place more weight on the
landscape-implied covariance relative to diagonal noise. The ratio should be
interpreted within a fixed template because rescaling $E(\theta)$ also changes
its meaning.

> **Keep `nu` front of mind.** As explained in
> `vignette("wishart-covariance", package = "terradish")`, `nu` acts as an
> *effective sample size*. It does not change the *point estimates* recovered
> from a given covariance matrix, but it controls their precision (standard
> errors scale as $1/\sqrt{\nu}$) and therefore the **statistical power** to
> detect a nonzero coefficient under the simulated model. Sample size and
> marker design are important study-design levers, but the effective `nu` may
> be smaller than the raw marker count because of linkage and shared
> population history. No marker-count rule automatically establishes it.
> Distinguish the value that generates variation (`nu`) from the value
> assumed when fitting (`nu_fit`).

## A single simulated data set

`simulate_covariance_response()` returns one covariance matrix (plus the
ingredients used to build it). It has the same matrix form as a genotype
covariance summary, but its sampling distribution is generated by the model.
Real genotype summaries need not follow that distribution.



Refitting estimates the parameters used to generate the data. One draw may
depart substantially from the generating values:



A single replicate only tells you what happened *once*. To characterize a
design you must repeat the draw many times.

## Separate generating information from assumed information

`nu` controls variation among simulated covariance matrices. `nu_fit` controls
the precision assumed by the fitted likelihood; its default is `nu`. Raising
`nu_fit` while keeping `nu` fixed does not create more informative data. It
can make intervals narrower and increase nominal detections while reducing
coverage. Evaluate that mismatch explicitly:



**How to read the output.** Compare fit rate, coverage, and power together.
Similar estimates with narrower intervals under larger `nu_fit` reflect the
assumed precision, not better recovery of the generating process. A high
detection rate accompanied by poor coverage is not a successful design.
Failed and nonconverged fits count as non-detections; inspect their frequency
before interpreting power. Coverage and bias summaries require checking which
replicates supplied valid estimates.

These are runnable study-design recipes rather than stored results. Long
power sweeps are not run during vignette builds, and no hidden precomputed
files are needed to render this guide. Save your configuration, seeds, full
assessment objects, package versions, and convergence diagnostics when running
them. Report Monte Carlo uncertainty: for a proportion `p` estimated from
`B` independent replicates, its approximate Monte Carlo standard error is
`sqrt(p * (1 - p) / B)`.

For covariance simulation, the generating `nu` must be at least the covariance
dimension required by `stats::rWishart()`. That simulation constraint does not
make raw marker counts appropriate fitted effective degrees of freedom.

## Sampling sites and placement

Compare both site count and placement under the same generating scenarios.
More sites can improve recovery, but redundant sites or weak covariate coverage
may add little information. Hold generating and fitting `nu` explicit in each
comparison, include weak-signal scenarios, and assess whether the fitted model
is numerically identifiable. Study-design conclusions remain internal to the
simulated model. Additional validation against misspecified, coalescent, or
individual-based processes is needed before translating them into field targets.

## Comparing sampling strategies and candidate models

`covariance_response_power()` automates the same loop across **sampling
designs**: it subsets the focal sites in the surface to a target sample size,
optionally using different placement strategies, simulates responses, refits
candidate models, and summarizes recovery. The graph is reused across designs, but repeated fitting can still be costly.

Here we compare three sample sizes under two placement strategies, and we fit
two candidate models per replicate: the true two-covariate model and a reduced
altitude-only model. This lets us measure both **parameter recovery** and
**model-selection** performance.




### Scenario-level summary

The `summary` table has one row per (strategy, sample size, model):



Key columns:

- **`fit_rate`**: fraction of replicates that converged. Values below 1 flag a
  design where the model is hard to fit (often too few sites).
- **`conductance_power`**: fraction of replicates whose fitted conductance
  surface has a Pearson correlation with the generating surface above
  `conductance_cor_threshold` (default 0.9). This measures recovery of the
  relative spatial pattern, not its absolute scale or an ecological mechanism.
  It is useful for spline models whose basis coefficients are not individually
  interpretable.
- **`mean_conductance_cor`**: average correlation between the fitted and true
  conductance surfaces.
- **`selected_AIC_rate`**: how often each model had the lowest AIC within the
  specified, comparable candidate set. For the `full` model, this is the rate
  at which the generating formula ranked first among those candidates.

### Parameter-level summary

`parameter_summary` reports per-coefficient power, bias, RMSE, and confidence
interval coverage:



What to look for:

- **`power`**: proportion of all simulated replicates in which the coefficient
  had the generating sign and a two-sided Wald p-value below `alpha`. Failed or
  boundary fits count as non-detections. Choose a target such as 0.8 before the
  assessment rather than treating it as a universal threshold.
- **`bias`**: average estimate minus the generating value. Persistent bias can
  reflect model misspecification, boundary behavior, optimization failures, or a
  design too small for the asymptotic approximation.
- **`rmse`**: typical estimation error; should shrink as sample size grows.
- **`coverage`**: fraction of 95% confidence intervals that contain the truth.
  Well-calibrated intervals give ~0.95. Coverage well below 0.95 means the
  reported uncertainty is too optimistic for that design.

## Interpreting and acting on the results

A design assessment usually answers one of three questions:

| Question | Column(s) to read | Decision rule |
|----------|-------------------|---------------|
| Enough sites? | `parameter_summary$power`, `conductance_power` | Pick the smallest sample size that reaches your predeclared recovery target under conservative scenarios. |
| Enough marker information? | power-vs-`nu` curves | Identify the smallest plausible effective `nu` past the power elbow under conservative scenarios. |
| Which site placement? | compare `strategy` rows | Prefer the strategy with higher power / surface correlation at equal cost. |
| Trustworthy intervals? | `parameter_summary$coverage` | Require coverage near 0.95 before trusting reported CIs. |

A few practical notes:

- **Increase `nsim`.** The recipes use small pilot counts; no power sweep is executed at build time.
  For a real assessment use at least 100-500 simulations per design so that
  power and coverage estimates are stable.
- **Match the simulation to your expectations.** The answer depends on
  `theta`, `tau`, and `sigma`. If you are unsure, run the assessment across a
  grid of plausible effect sizes and $\tau / \sigma$ ratios, then report the
  range.
- **Use and report defensible `nu` values.** Markers in linkage disequilibrium
  are not independent, so the effective `nu` can be smaller than the raw marker
  count. Planning with an optimistic `nu` overstates precision and power. Use
  a justified range and report sensitivity to both generating `nu` and
  assumed `nu_fit`.
- **Spline and Gaussian models.** For non-linear conductance models the
  individual coefficients are not interpretable; lean on
  `conductance_power` and `mean_conductance_cor` rather than per-parameter
  power. Pass the relevant factory via `conductance_model` and `fit_models`.

## Why splines for conductance?

The default `terradish` conductance model is **log-linear**:

$$C_i = \exp(\theta_1 x_{i1} + \theta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots)$$

This model represents a monotone fitted association for each landscape covariate: does relative conductance go up or down as the covariate increases, conditional on the other predictors? That constrained form is useful when a monotone relationship is scientifically defensible.

But ecological responses to landscape features are often more complex. Consider:

- **Elevation**: A prior hypothesis may predict the strongest connectivity at intermediate elevations rather than at either extreme.
- **Slope**: A prior hypothesis may predict little change until terrain passes a threshold.
- **Temperature**: Many ectotherms have a thermal optimum; conductance may be highest at intermediate temperatures and fall off steeply in both directions.
- **Canopy density**: A prior hypothesis may predict lower connectivity at both low and high canopy density.

None of these shapes can be captured by a single linear term on the log scale. A negative coefficient gives a declining fitted conductance association; a positive coefficient gives an increasing association. There is no room for an interior optimum.

`smooth_loglinear_conductance()` extends the log-linear model by expanding selected covariates into **spline basis functions**. The model structure is still:

$$C_i = \exp(B_i \boldsymbol{\theta})$$

where $B_i$ is a row of the **expanded design matrix** for raster cell $i$. For covariates not wrapped in `s()`, $B_i$ contains the raw value. For covariates wrapped in `s()`, $B_i$ contains several basis-function evaluations instead of one raw value. The optimizer still sees an ordinary log-linear model; the splines live entirely inside the design matrix, which is computed once and then treated as fixed. These are **unpenalized**
regression splines with a chosen number of coefficients, not smooths whose
complexity is estimated by a smoothing penalty. Knots, boundary knots, and
column-centering offsets are retained from training. Plotting and new-surface
prediction reuse that basis rather than selecting new knots. Centering removes
an arbitrary constant conductance multiplier; it does not change the fitted
shape.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What are spline basis functions?

A spline is a piecewise polynomial function that is constrained to be smooth (continuous and differentiable) at the join points, called **knots**. Instead of estimating a single slope for altitude, we estimate a weighted combination of several basis functions, each capturing part of the altitude range.

The figure below shows centered natural spline and B-spline basis functions look like for a covariate that runs from −2 to 2 (as after `scale_covariates()`):



Each basis column covers a different part of the covariate range. The optimizer assigns a coefficient to each column; the weighted sum of all columns defines the shape of the association. A hump in the middle of the covariate range is captured by upweighting the middle basis columns relative to the edge ones.

### Natural splines vs. B-splines

Both produce smooth curves, but they differ in behavior outside the observed data range:

| Property | Natural spline (`ns`) | B-spline (`bs`) |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Behavior at edges | **Linear** beyond boundary knots | Polynomial (degree-dependent); can extrapolate wildly |
| Partition of unity | No | Yes (basis sums to 1 everywhere) |
| Parameter count for `df = k` | `k` columns | `k` columns (knots placed to give `k`) |
| Default degree | N/A (cubic, fixed) | Cubic (`degree = 3`); user-adjustable |
| Best for | General use; safe extrapolation | When you know the curve may be non-linear at the edges too |

For most landscape-genetics applications, **natural splines are likely the safer default**. The landscape feature you observe at sampling sites is what matters; extrapolation is usually not meaningful for raster-based conductance models because the graph only covers cells with observed covariate values. Still, B-splines are useful when you have strong prior knowledge that the edge behavior is non-linear, or when you want finer control over the spline degree.

### How many basis functions (df / k)?

More basis functions → more shape flexibility → more parameters to estimate. With sparse spatial genetic data, overfitting is a real risk. Guidelines:

- **df = 3 or 4** is a good starting point for most covariates. This allows a single hump, valley, or threshold-like shape.
- **df = 2** is a low-flexibility nonlinear curve; it adds one parameter over the linear model and can represent simple curvature.
- **df ≥ 6** should be reserved for cases where both the data and a clear ecological hypothesis support it; compare against simpler predeclared models with held-out prediction.
- Use common cross-validation folds for the candidate shapes. AICc is a
  secondary diagnostic under its focal-site sample-size convention; it does
  not make all site pairs independent or calibrate an uncertain Wishart `nu`.

## B-spline degree exploration

B-splines allow control over the polynomial degree separately from the number of basis columns. Degree 1 is piecewise linear, degree 2 is quadratic, and degree 3 (the default) is cubic. Higher degrees produce smoother but more globally coupled curves.

## Practical recommendations

### When to use spline conductance

Use `smooth_loglinear_conductance()` when:

1.  **There is a prior ecological reason** to expect a non-linear landscape association, e.g. an intermediate optimum, a threshold, or a well-documented hump-shaped response in the literature.
2.  **The linear model's marginal association is implausible**: e.g. a strong negative altitude coefficient that would predict near-zero conductance at high elevations where sampling actually occurs.
3.  **The design can support the extra parameters.** Pairwise observations are dependent, so do not justify spline complexity by counting all site pairs as independent replicates. Evaluate held-out prediction, stability, focal-site support, and simulation under plausible scenarios.

### When to stay with the linear model

- Designs with sparse focal-site or covariate support relative to the proposed spline complexity.
- No prior hypothesis about a non-linear association; treat splines as exploratory rather than confirmatory.
- The spline and linear models give similar held-out scores; parsimony favors the simpler model.

### Checking for overfitting

Even when AICc improves, inspect these warning signs:

- **Wild oscillation** in the marginal curve, especially with sign reversals at the edges of the covariate range.
- **Very large basis coefficients** relative to their standard errors.
- **Near-singular design matrix** or optimizer warnings about convergence.
- **Large discrepancy between ns and bs** at the same df/k: if the two spline families give qualitatively different shapes, neither has strong support.

## Overview

The standard `terradish` workflow uses a pre-computed **distance
matrix** (e.g. F~ST~) as the genetic response. Two measurement models,
`leastsquares` and `mlpe`, accept such matrices and differ only in how
they handle the non-independence of pairwise observations.

When raw individual-level genotype data are available, you can retain a
population covariance summary rather than beginning from a precomputed
distance matrix:

1.  Summarize the genetic data as a **covariance matrix** between
    populations.
2. Fit the resistance model with `wishart_covariance`, using a declared
   effective degrees of freedom, `nu`.

Both Wishart interfaces use the **contrast likelihood**. For an orthonormal
site-contrast matrix `Q`, the covariance likelihood uses `Q' S Q` and the
corresponding fitted contrast covariance. Components in the constant-site
direction are excluded. Centering `S` therefore leaves this likelihood
unchanged. The previous full-rank formulation was removed because that
constant direction depends on arbitrary centering conventions and does not
provide the intended resistance information.

`dist_from_cov(S)` retains the covariance information used by this likelihood.
Covariance and matching squared-distance inputs are two representations of
the same contrast model. Neither is inherently more informative. Begin with
`vignette("getting-started", package = "terradish")` for the graph workflow.

| Model | Input `S` | Requires `nu` | Correlation structure |
|----|----|----|----|
| `leastsquares` | distance matrix | no | none |
| `mlpe` | distance matrix | no | shared-site |
| `generalized_wishart` | admissible squared-distance matrix | yes | generalized Wishart |
| `wishart_covariance` | **covariance matrix** | yes | Wishart |

This vignette covers:

- Converting different types of raw genetic data to a covariance matrix
  with `cov_from_genetic_data()` (and `cov_from_biallelic()` for
  biallelic SNPs).

- Fitting an IBR model with `wishart_covariance`.

## Choosing `nu`

`nu` is a positive effective degrees-of-freedom value supplied by the analyst.
It is not estimated by `terradish`, and it is not fixed automatically by the
number of markers used to build `S`. Linkage creates dependence among nearby
markers; shared population history can create dependence even among unlinked
markers. Counting SNPs, loci, or within-locus allele contrasts does not by
itself establish effective replication.

A block jackknife can assess how marker blocks contribute to variability.
Because it need not capture all dependence due to shared history, regard its
implied information as an upper-bound guide rather than a complete calibration.
Describe the blocks and the uncertainty sources the procedure represents.

Declare a primary value with its rationale and a range of plausible values.
Use `terradish_rescale_nu()` for sensitivity of intervals and likelihood-based
comparisons. Use fixed-domain cross-validation to assess conductance terms
and shape. A stable result over a declared range is evidence of sensitivity
within that range, not proof that the primary value is correct.

If only a published F~ST~ matrix is available, a reported locus count does not
make that response Wishart. F~ST~ is a ratio estimator; consider MLPE for such
dissimilarities and evaluate its shared-site assumptions. See
`vignette("model-comparison", package = "terradish")` for predictive comparisons.

### Key arguments

| Argument | Default | Effect |
|----|----|----|
| `groups` | `NULL` | Population vector; if `NULL`, individual-level covariance is returned |
| `input` | `"features"` | `"features"` for numeric matrices; `"allele_calls"` for allele-call columns |
| `loci` | `NULL` | Required for `"allele_calls"`: maps each column to its locus |
| `center` | `TRUE` | Centers each feature by its global mean before covariance calculation |
| `scale` | `TRUE` | Scales each feature by its global standard deviation; drops constant features |
| `diagonal` | `"auto"` | `"auto"` selects `"gower"`; `"within"` replaces the diagonal with within-population variance |
| `normalize` | `"none"` | `"features"` divides covariance by number of retained features |

The default grouped-data construction uses `diagonal = "gower"`. Replacing
that diagonal with `diagonal = "within"` changes the covariance estimand and
can change fitted signs; the supplied release simulations recovered the
expected signs with Gower diagonals but not with the within-population
replacement. Retain `within` only for a separately justified construction,
and compare its effect explicitly. It may also produce an indefinite matrix.



**How to read the output.** A centered covariance has a zero eigenvalue in
the constant direction, so strict positive definiteness of the full matrix
is not the target. Tiny negative values may reflect rounding. Substantive
negative contrast eigenvalues require investigation; changing the diagonal
is not a neutral repair.

  formula.

### Understanding the nuisance parameters

The `wishart_covariance` measurement model fits two nuisance parameters
(`phi`):

- **`tau`** (≥ 0): a nonnegative weight applied to the
  conductance-implied covariance `E`. If `tau` ≈ 0, there is no
  detectable graph-covariance signal under this response and model. Its
  magnitude is not comparable to the Gaussian distance-model `tau`,
  which is a log precision.
- **`sigma`** (log-scale): a scalar nugget added as `exp(sigma) * I` to
  the fitted covariance matrix. It captures diagonal variation not
  represented by the graph kernel, including sampling variation and
  model mismatch. It is not the Gaussian raster-smoothing scale and is
  not a direct estimate of drift or population size.

The covariance kernel is `Sigma = tau * E(theta) + exp(sigma) * I`.
The likelihood evaluates its projection into the site-contrast subspace.

where `E(theta)` is the generalized inverse of the graph Laplacian
evaluated at the estimated conductance parameters `theta`.

## The role of `nu`: an effective sample size

`nu` is the most consequential setting in a covariance-based fit. The
parameter-dependent Wishart objective is multiplied by `nu / 2`, so
`nu` is a global multiplier of the objective surface, which has three
separate consequences.

**1. The likelihood optimum does not depend on `nu`.** Scaling an
objective by a positive constant does not move its optimum, so the
theoretical point estimates of `theta`, `tau`, and `sigma` are invariant
to `nu`. Separate numerical fits should agree up to optimization
tolerance:



**2. Standard errors scale as 1 / sqrt(`nu`).** The Hessian scales with
`nu`, so the asymptotic covariance scales with 1 / `nu` and every
interior standard error scales with 1 / sqrt(`nu`). Boundary inference
requires separate care. Under the model, more
approximately independent marker information produces tighter intervals:



**3. Model selection and hypothesis tests depend strongly on `nu`.** The
log-likelihood scales linearly with `nu`, but the AIC/BIC complexity
penalty (and the chi-squared reference distribution for a
likelihood-ratio test) does not. With large `nu` the penalty becomes
negligible and extra covariates are almost always retained; with small
`nu`, parsimony dominates. The *same data* can favor a simpler or a more
complex model purely through the choice of `nu`.

Report how intervals and model comparisons depend on `nu`. Rescaling
does not re-estimate conductance, so this sensitivity is inexpensive and
separates the fitted surface from the assumed precision. Use the same `nu`
for every candidate in a comparison. Neither a high AIC weight nor a small
nominal LRT p-value establishes that `nu` represents independent information.

## Preparing a generalized-Wishart squared-distance response

The generalized-Wishart likelihood expects a squared-distance response
${D = d^2}$, not an arbitrary symmetric dissimilarity matrix. For ${n}$
sites, `terradish` forms the centered Gram matrix

$$B = -\frac{1}{2} H D H, \qquad
H = I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T},$$

and requires ${B}$ to be positive semidefinite within a relative
numerical tolerance. This geometry is the mathematical support condition
for the likelihood. It is separate from whether the selected genetic
summary is biologically appropriate for the sampling design and
scientific question.

Use `check_distance_response()` on the matrix that you actually intend
to fit:



**How to read the diagnostic:** `admissible = TRUE` means the implied
centered Gram matrix is positive semidefinite within tolerance. A very
small negative `relative_min_eigenvalue` can reflect floating-point
rounding. `n_negative` counts only eigenvalues that are more negative
than the scaled tolerance. `terradish()` performs this check
automatically before any generalized-Wishart fit and stops on
substantive violations.

The checker does not select a genetic distance for you. Choose the
response based on the biology, marker type, sampling design, and
intended interpretation, then test the realized matrix. The name of a
distance measure is not enough because scaling, locus aggregation,
transformations, and missing-data handling can change the resulting
geometry.

### Potentially relevant distance measures available in R

The table is a navigation aid, not a list of recommended or
interchangeable responses. Functions can differ in their scaling, input
class, treatment of missing data, and whether they operate on
individuals or populations. Confirm what the selected function returns.
If it returns ordinary Euclidean distances ${d}$, square the matrix to
obtain ${D = d^2}$ before calling `check_distance_response()` or fitting
`generalized_wishart`.

| Candidate measure | Example R implementations | Guidance for generalized Wishart |
|----|----|----|
| Edwards/angular and Cavalli-Sforza-Edwards chord distances | `adegenet::dist.genpop(method = 2)`, `poppr::edwards.dist()`, `hierfstat::genet.dist(method = "Dch")` | The `adegenet` manual labels method 2 Euclidean. Cavalli-Sforza-Edwards chord distance is one potentially relevant candidate, not a required or preferred response. Verify the exact implementation and square ordinary distances before checking. |
| Reynolds distance | `adegenet::dist.genpop(method = 3)`, `poppr::reynolds.dist()` | The `adegenet` manual labels its implementation Euclidean. Verify the realized matrix and its scaling. |
| Rogers distance | `adegenet::dist.genpop(method = 4)`, `poppr::rogers.dist()`, `hierfstat::genet.dist(method = "Dr")` | The `adegenet` manual labels method 4 Euclidean. Square ordinary distances and check the resulting matrix. |
| Nei distances | `adegenet::dist.genpop(method = 1)`, `poppr::nei.dist()`, variants from `hierfstat::genet.dist()` | These names cover different formulas. The `adegenet` manual labels method 1 non-Euclidean, so do not assume admissibility. |
| Prevosti distance | `adegenet::dist.genpop(method = 5)`, `poppr::prevosti.dist()`, `hierfstat::genet.dist(method = "Cp")` | The `adegenet` manual labels method 5 non-Euclidean. Do not assume admissibility. |
| Pairwise F~ST~ or F~ST~/(1 - F~ST~) | `hierfstat::pairwise.WCfst()`, `hierfstat::pairwise.neifst()` | These are common landscape-genetic responses, but they have no general squared-Euclidean guarantee. F~ST~ is a ratio estimator without a Wishart sampling justification; passing a geometry check is insufficient. Consider `mlpe` and assess its assumptions. |
| Euclidean, chord, or Hellinger distances on transformed numeric features | `stats::dist()`, `vegan::vegdist(method = "euclidean")`, `vegan::vegdist(method = "chord")`, `vegan::vegdist(method = "hellinger")` | Geometry depends on the feature representation and transformation. Scientific appropriateness remains study-specific. |

`ade4::is.euclid()` provides an optional independent diagnostic for
ordinary distances. Because it squares its input internally, the
equivalent call for an already squared generalized-Wishart response `D`
is:



Do not call `ade4::is.euclid(as.dist(D))` for this purpose because that
tests whether ${D^2}$, rather than ${D}$, has the required geometry.
Functions such as `ade4::cailliez()` and `ade4::lingoes()` can make an

## Compare with `generalized_wishart`

`generalized_wishart` uses the corresponding generalized Wishart
formulation for an admissible **squared-distance** representation of the
same genetic data. Here the response was obtained from the covariance
matrix with `dist_from_cov()` and checked above:



When the covariance and admissible squared-distance representations
contain the same information under identical centering, scaling, sites,
and `nu`, their fitted conductance parameters should agree up to
numerical tolerance. The AIC comparison is not meaningful across the two
models because the responses differ. Standard errors should only be
compared after confirming that the transformation is lossless and the
effective information assumptions are identical.

## Choosing between covariance and distance input

| Consideration | Guidance |
|----|----|
| Raw genotype data available | Construct and document an appropriate covariance summary; assess filtering and unequal sampling. |
| Covariance and its matching squared distances available | Both Wishart interfaces use the same contrast likelihood. Choose the representation that makes preprocessing clearest. |
| Only a precomputed dissimilarity is available | Geometry is necessary but insufficient for Wishart sampling; consider MLPE for generic genetic distances. |
| Site-specific sampling variance matters | A common nugget does not fully address unequal sampling; examine design and sensitivity. |
| Effective information is uncertain | Declare a range of `nu`, rescale inference, and assess conductance complexity with held-out prediction. |

### Environmental differences between sites

`pairwise_covariates()` combines geographic distances and endpoint environmental
differences in one design. Both `mlpe_covariates()` and `wishart_covariates()`
accept that design, while linking it to different response models. For Wishart,
the nonnegative environmental kernel weights describe conditional contributions
to covariance structure. They do not identify a particular biological
mechanism. See `vignette("ibe-ibr-workflow", package = "terradish")` for a
worked comparison, conditional conductance estimates, and IBE:IBR ratios.
